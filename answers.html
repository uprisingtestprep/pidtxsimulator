Pediatric Infectious Diseases Fundamentals & Review

Answer Keys and Explanations for every Board-Style Question, organized by chapter. Question stems and answer choices are printed in each chapter of the book.

Chapter 1: Principles of Pediatric Infection and Immune Development

Question 1

Absence of cough and rash does not exclude invasive disease, and urine testing cannot exclude infection in blood or cerebrospinal fluid.
Question 2

Why the other choices are wrong: Group child care and early childhood otitis media are common exposures rather than proof of immune deficiency. Why the other choices are wrong: Devices provide a surface where organisms can form biofilm and persist. A supple neck does not exclude shunt infection or ventriculitis. A rash is not required, and headache is a common symptom when shunt function or cerebrospinal fluid inflammation is abnormal.

Chapter 2: Fever in Infants and Children

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Young infants with fever can have serious bacterial infection without focal signs. A mildly elevated temperature and absence of cough do not make the child low risk. A urine test alone cannot exclude bloodstream or central nervous system infection, and delayed follow-up is unsafe for a child with poor feeding and reduced interaction.
Question 2
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: Warm extremities, normal refill, and playful behavior suggest reasonable perfusion. A response to antipyretic does not prove the illness is benign. Mottled skin, cool limbs, delayed refill, and altered alertness are signs of poor perfusion that require urgent evaluation. Heart rate normally changes with crying and sleep.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Malaria and other travel-associated infections are linked to place, date, incubation period, and specific exposures. A previous winter illness does not explain recent travel risk. Medicine brand and school staffing may affect care logistics but do not guide urgent testing for malaria or another imported infection.

Chapter 3: Clinical Reasoning and Diagnostic Stewardship

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A culture is useful when its result changes care and when it is obtained with enough volume and proper technique. Extra testing does not make a poor specimen valuable. Antibiotics should not be delayed dangerously, but cultures should be collected before treatment when feasible. A blood count cannot replace culture information.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Culture information should be used to narrow therapy when the child is stable and source control is adequate. Continuing a broad regimen unnecessarily increases harm. Stopping treatment immediately is not appropriate for a bacterial infection that still needs therapy. Adding coverage for organisms not implicated by the culture usually broadens without benefit.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Joint fluid can provide a pathogen and supports source control. It does not exclude every noninfectious disease, and monitoring remains necessary. Transient synovitis can overlap with infection, especially after a viral illness. Imaging helps show fluid and complications but cannot reliably replace fluid examination.

Chapter 4: Microbiology and Rapid Diagnostics

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Paired cultures with accurate labels help distinguish a line-associated infection from a bloodstream infection with another source. The peripheral culture is not infallible. Two samples reduce some uncertainty but do not eliminate contamination. A line infection can cause fever without an abnormal exit site.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: A virus can cause or contribute to the illness without excluding bacterial coinfection or a complication. Hypoxia and focal findings require clinical assessment. Molecular tests are clinically useful but need context. Broad antibiotics are not indicated for every positive viral result.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Repeated growth from multiple sites or repeated cultures supports true infection. A virulent organism with shock and endocarditis findings is highly concerning. One positive culture from several samples, especially with a common skin organism and no supporting device evidence, may represent contamination, although the child must still be evaluated.

Chapter 5: Antimicrobial Pharmacology and Pediatric Dosing

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: The volume changes when the concentration changes, even if the intended dose in milligrams stays the same. Recalculation and verification prevent a tenfold error. Taste does not justify an altered amount. A concentration change can be a normal product substitution and should be reconciled rather than assumed to be an error.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Newborn physiology changes rapidly, including fluid compartments, protein binding, enzyme activity, and kidney filtration. These changes affect exposure and toxicity. Weight can be measured accurately. The immune system does not simply destroy antibiotics, and dosing decisions require neonatal guidance.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: The timing and features of the reaction determine whether it was severe and whether the allergy label is credible. Palatability and school administration are practical issues but do not define allergy. Exposure to an unrelated antibiotic does not clarify the reaction.

Chapter 6: Antimicrobial Stewardship and Resistance

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Thick mucus and low-grade fever do not prove bacterial infection. Supportive care with clear return precautions is appropriate for an uncomplicated viral cold. A blood culture is not indicated routinely for a well child with a cold. Timing of doses does not make an unnecessary antibiotic useful.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: De-escalation uses the organism and susceptibilities to choose a narrower effective treatment. The child still needs therapy and follow-up. Broad continuation increases harm. Adding broad coverage without evidence can increase resistance and adverse effects.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Source control removes infected material and improves antibiotic effectiveness, often allowing a shorter course. Antibiotics may still be needed. Drainage does not turn a pathogen into a harmless colonizer. Source control is not a reason to broaden or prolong therapy without a clinical indication.

Chapter 7: Infection Prevention and Immunization

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Missed doses usually do not require restarting a series. Valid doses should be documented and remaining doses completed using the catch-up schedule. Waiting increases preventable risk. School requirements are not a complete immunization plan.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Children with immune suppression, absent splenic function, or invasive devices have increased risk from specific organisms. They may need additional vaccines, prophylaxis, and a written fever plan. Routine prevention is still important for the other children, but it does not require the same specialized program.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Gloves reduce contact with body fluids but can become contaminated and can tear. Hand hygiene is required before and after contact and after glove removal. Transmission precautions are not limited to diagnosed resistant organisms, because many infections are unrecognized.

Chapter 8: Neonatal Infections

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Apnea and poor feeding are dangerous newborn signs that can reflect invasive infection. Normal oxygen saturation does not exclude sepsis or meningitis. Prematurity may change the baseline, but a clear deterioration still requires urgent assessment and treatment.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: The inside of a catheter can be colonized even when the external site looks clean. A dressing reduces but does not eliminate risk. Newborns can develop infection without a rash, and fever or apnea may be the main clue.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Congenital infection can affect growth, brain size, hearing, eyes, liver, skin, and blood counts. Vernix is a normal newborn finding. One sneeze or a normal examination alone is not a concern, but no screening program replaces evaluation when a concerning pattern appears.

Chapter 9: Sepsis and Toxic Shock Syndromes

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Children may compensate for shock before blood pressure falls. Mottling, delayed refill, and altered mental status require urgent treatment. Observation and delayed antibiotics are unsafe. Blood pressure is useful but not the only measure of circulation.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A retained infected material can continue releasing toxin and sustaining infection. Removal is part of source control, but supportive care and antibiotics may still be required. Antibiotics can enter tissue, but they cannot replace removal of a focus. Leaving the packing in place can prolong severe illness.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Fluid is a treatment with benefits and harms. New respiratory distress may reflect fluid overload or worsening lung injury. Reassessment should guide fluids, respiratory support, and vasoactive therapy. A normal blood pressure does not mean the child is ready for discharge.

Chapter 10: Upper Respiratory Tract Infections

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: A routine cold is usually viral and does not need antibiotics or a blood culture. Supportive care with clear return precautions is appropriate. Some cough medicines are unsafe or ineffective in young children, so age-specific guidance is required.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Narrow therapy is preferred for confirmed streptococcal pharyngitis. Broad coverage adds resistance and adverse effects without benefit. Treatment reduces symptoms and prevents some complications. A topical spray does not reliably treat the infection.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Drooling, a muffled voice, neck stiffness, and trismus suggest a deep neck infection or airway threat. A mild cold and a nighttime cough are common. A negative test does not itself indicate a deep infection, but clinical deterioration requires reassessment.

Chapter 11: Otitis Media Sinusitis and Mastoiditis

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Redness alone is nonspecific. Acute otitis media requires middle-ear fluid and acute inflammatory signs. Wax does not establish infection. External ear canal inflammation is a different diagnosis from middle-ear infection.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Postauricular swelling and outward displacement suggest mastoiditis, which can extend to a subperiosteal abscess or the brain. Urgent specialist evaluation is required. Home observation and symptomatic treatment are unsafe for this presentation.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Sinus infection can spread into the orbit, causing severe infection, vision loss, or intracranial extension. Routine viral illness does not explain proptosis or painful eye movement. Bone does not provide an absolute barrier, and these eye findings require urgent evaluation.

Chapter 12: Pneumonia and Lower Respiratory Tract Infections

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Hypoxemia, respiratory distress, and dehydration indicate physiologic compromise. A mild illness with normal oxygen and good intake can often be managed at home. Past colds do not set current severity, and a child with a normal examination and playful behavior is less concerning.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Cultures can be useful but are not high-yield in every mild case. Severity, hospitalization, device status, immune risk, and complications increase their value. A blood count does not prove sterility. Cultures are best obtained before antibiotics when safe.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Persistent fever and worsening distress suggest a complication or inadequate treatment. Empyema, abscess, and necrotizing disease may require imaging and source control. Taste is not a cause of progressive respiratory failure, and worsening illness needs prompt reassessment.

Chapter 13: Bronchiolitis and Viral Respiratory Disease

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Typical bronchiolitis is managed supportively. Antibiotics and steroids are not routine for uncomplicated viral disease. Cough suppressants are unsafe or ineffective in young infants. A chest tube is used for a specific pleural complication.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Apnea is a dangerous sign, especially in a young or premature infant. Urgent assessment and monitoring are required. A bronchodilator is not a safe substitute for escalation. Oxygen and breathing status must be assessed.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Feeding is work for an infant with narrowed airways, and poor intake can reflect fatigue or respiratory compromise. Good feeding is useful but does not exclude later deterioration. Nutrition and fluids remain important, and supportive feeding may need to change route or volume.

Chapter 14: Influenza RSV and Emerging Respiratory Viruses

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Early antiviral treatment can benefit high-risk children. Antibiotics do not treat influenza itself. Testing can guide treatment and isolation. Aspirin is avoided in children because of Reye syndrome risk.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Prematurity and chronic lung disease increase the risk of severe bronchiolitis, apnea, and feeding failure. Mild illness in an otherwise healthy child is less concerning. Age and underlying conditions change the risk from the same virus.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A biphasic course can indicate bacterial superinfection or another complication. Persistent fever and focal findings need reassessment. Viral testing does not exclude bacterial disease, but antibiotics are not automatically required for every viral illness.

Chapter 15: Tuberculosis and Nontuberculous Mycobacteria

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: A positive immune test indicates sensitization. A normal examination and imaging may fit latent infection, though active disease must be assessed. Preventive treatment can reduce progression in young or high-risk children. A negative test would not prove no exposure in every host.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Children usually acquire tuberculosis from adults or older adolescents rather than transmitting it widely. Contact investigation finds the source and protects other vulnerable people. It does not replace treating the child, and it should happen promptly.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Young age and immune suppression are major risk factors for progression after infection. Recent infection also increases risk. A negative test and no exposure do not establish latent infection. Routine vaccines do not replace tuberculosis prevention or treatment.

Chapter 16: Meningitis and Encephalitis

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Young infants often lack meningismus. Poor feeding and lethargy are concerning for sepsis or meningitis. A soft fontanelle and a cry do not exclude infection. Newborns may be hypothermic rather than febrile.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Treatment must not be delayed for a procedure that cannot be performed safely. Blood cultures and clinical evaluation still provide information. Discharge is unsafe, and steroids do not replace antimicrobial therapy.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Meningitis can cause hearing loss, seizures, hydrocephalus, and developmental effects. Follow-up should match the child's risks. Routine weekly lumbar puncture is not indicated for every child, and infection does not cancel the need for routine vaccination.

Chapter 17: Brain Abscess and Focal Central Nervous System Infection

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Sinus infection can extend through bone into the orbit or brain. A focal lesion needs specialist management and source assessment. Blood cultures and lesion sampling can identify the organism. Steroids do not replace antimicrobial and source-control treatment.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A focal lesion can raise pressure and change cerebrospinal fluid dynamics, making a lumbar puncture dangerous. The decision is individualized by specialists. Lumbar puncture can diagnose some central nervous system infections, but it is not safe in every child and is not necessary after complete resolution alone.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: New neurologic deficits, altered consciousness, and signs of increased pressure require urgent specialist review. Stable findings may be monitored by a team, but deterioration changes the pace. A mild rhinovirus or a normal follow-up finding does not indicate an emergency by itself.

Chapter 18: Skin and Soft Tissue Infections

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A fluctuant lesion suggests an abscess, and drainage is often definitive. Moisturizer does not treat a collection. Amputation is not routine. Cultures may be useful, especially for recurrent, severe, or unusual infection, even though the skin surface is colonized.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Necrotizing infection causes tissue injury that is more severe than the surface suggests. Systemic toxicity and disproportionate pain are red flags. A crusted lesion may be impetigo, and itching alone is usually not a deep infection.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A full examination finds spread, portals, complications, and systemic illness. Skin infection can cause fever or be associated with a deeper process. The history and depth assessment guide treatment. Antibiotics should follow a diagnosis and source-control plan.

Chapter 19: Bone and Joint Infections

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Fever and a joint that cannot move require urgent exclusion of septic arthritis. Transient synovitis may resemble infection but is a diagnosis of exclusion in a febrile, unwell child. Delay can damage the joint. Antibiotics alone do not replace aspiration and source control.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Early bone infection may not yet produce visible destruction. A normal radiograph does not exclude disease. Osteomyelitis involves bone and marrow, and MRI can show early marrow and soft tissue changes.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Drainage removes infected material and reduces pressure, supporting recovery. Antibiotics are still needed. Source control does not depend on a negative blood culture, and untreated arthritis can cause lasting damage.

Chapter 20: Bacteremia Endocarditis and Intravascular Infections

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A single positive culture may be contamination, but device risk and symptoms matter. Repeat cultures and assessment improve interpretation. Routine long treatment for every positive result is not justified, and ignoring the result is unsafe. Device removal is a major decision that requires context.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Persistent bacteremia, a new murmur, and embolic findings are classic clues. Mild viral symptoms or a resolving rash do not suggest endocarditis. A negative culture in a low-risk child is not a reason to pursue echocardiography without another indication.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Biofilm and infected hardware can persist despite active antibiotics. Catheter salvage is possible in selected cases, so removal is not automatic for every culture. A healed exit site does not exclude internal colonization.

Chapter 21: Urinary Tract and Renal Infections

Question 1
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Bag cultures are prone to contamination. A positive result needs confirmation and clinical correlation. A negative bag result can be useful for screening. Long therapy without a confirmed diagnosis creates avoidable harm.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Imaging is reserved for cases where anatomy or complications could change management. Routine imaging is not needed for every uncomplicated episode. A negative urinalysis and no symptoms do not justify a urinary imaging workup.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: A full rectum can affect bladder emptying and contribute to urinary stasis. Bowel care is part of prevention but does not replace appropriate treatment for acute kidney infection. Blood contamination is not the mechanism.

Chapter 22: Gastroenteritis and Enteric Infections

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Oral rehydration and continued feeding are central for a child who can drink. Antibiotics are not routine for watery diarrhea. Antidiarrheal medicines can cause harm. Fluid replacement is necessary to prevent dehydration.
Question 2
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Blood, high fever, travel, outbreak exposure, and severe illness increase the value of stool testing. Mild short-lived disease usually does not need a pathogen-specific test. Clinical hydration remains important in every case.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Slowing stool can retain pathogens and toxins, and some medicines have serious adverse effects. They do not treat dehydration. They are not a treatment for severe colitis, and they do not replace hydration assessment.

Chapter 23: Intra-abdominal Infections and Hepatitis

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Antibiotics cannot reliably cure a large infected collection. Drainage or surgery reduces bacterial burden. A persistent collection can sustain sepsis, and follow-up must confirm improvement and resolution.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Rigidity, shock, and free air suggest perforation or peritonitis. Mild symptoms with a normal examination are less urgent but still require context. A resolving stomachache and normal findings do not indicate a surgical emergency.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: The differential includes infections, drugs, biliary disease, and inherited conditions. Treatment differs by cause. Jaundice requires assessment, and severe liver dysfunction can cause encephalopathy and coagulopathy.

Chapter 24: Viral Exanthems and Herpesvirus Infections

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Petechiae with systemic illness can indicate meningococcemia or another emergency. Antihistamines and observation are unsafe. Vital signs and perfusion assessment are essential, and skin findings do not replace urgent clinical evaluation.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Neonatal herpes can cause skin, eye, mouth, brain, or disseminated infection. Vesicles and poor feeding require urgent assessment and treatment. A benign rash does not explain lethargy, and varicella in a newborn can also be serious. Food allergy does not explain vesicles with systemic illness.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Antibiotics do not treat Epstein-Barr virus and may cause a rash. They are used when a bacterial complication is documented or strongly suspected. Mononucleosis commonly causes fever and pharyngitis, and viral infection does not eliminate the possibility of a bacterial complication.

Chapter 25: HIV Sexually Transmitted Infections and Adolescent Care

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Maternal HIV antibody may remain in the infant without infection. Virologic tests detect the virus and are used in the appropriate age window. Perinatal transmission can occur, and pediatric testing follows a specific algorithm.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Prompt treatment reduces long-term reproductive harm. Other emergencies can mimic pelvic infection and still need evaluation. Pelvic infection can cause serious complications. Confidential care supports disclosure and partner treatment.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Privacy and respectful communication improve trust and care. Disclosure should follow local law and the adolescent's safety and preferences. A useful history includes partners and risk but should be gathered sensitively, without making testing a condition.

Chapter 26: Fungal and Opportunistic Infections

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Persistent fever and a new lesion in a high-risk host suggest fungal or another opportunistic infection. A new lung lesion needs assessment. Blood cultures are insensitive for molds. Broadening antibacterial therapy without addressing the host and lesion is not sufficient.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Dead tissue and poor blood supply limit drug delivery. Surgical debridement is essential, but antifungal therapy may still be required. Mold infections can cause necrosis. Imaging supports, but does not replace, careful examination.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Severe immune suppression, prolonged low neutrophils, transplant, and invasive devices increase fungal risk. Mild viral symptoms in a healthy child do not. A resolving rash is not a fungal risk factor by itself.

Chapter 27: Parasitic and Helminthic Infections

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Malaria can progress rapidly, and prophylaxis is not perfect. Urgent testing and severity assessment are required. A routine delay is unsafe, and stool testing alone does not address the travel-related fever.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Intermittent shedding can cause false-negative examinations. Multiple focused samples improve detection when the exposure and syndrome fit. Eosinophilia is a clue, not a reason to skip stool evaluation. Parasites can cause diarrhea and growth failure.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Safe water and sanitation match the fecal-oral transmission risk. Travel alone does not justify broad treatment. The details of location, time, and exposure determine prevention. A mosquito net does not prevent shellfish-associated infection.

Chapter 28: Congenital Perinatal and Transplant-Associated Infections

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Congenital infections can affect hearing and development after the newborn period. A normal examination does not exclude infection. Hearing should be monitored according to the infant's risk and screening results, even without a rash.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Transplant prevention is a coordinated, time-dependent plan. Prophylaxis should not be stopped without the transplant team. Continuous broad antibiotics create resistance and toxicity. Selected vaccines are important and should be timed with specialist guidance.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Maternal antibody can remain in the newborn and cause a positive antibody result without infection. Virologic testing and age-specific algorithms clarify the diagnosis. Sterile-site cultures and confirmed molecular results are not explained by passive maternal antibody.

Chapter 29: Travel Refugee and Globally Mobile Child Health

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Malaria is a time-critical diagnosis, so travel details and urgent testing come first. Past colds do not explain recent travel fever. Medicine preference is practical but not urgent. A febrile returning traveler should not wait without assessment.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Screening detects possible disease, not a complete diagnosis. Confirmation and treatment planning matter. A well appearance does not rule out latent infection or parasites. Results should be explained sensitively with a clear follow-up plan.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Travel prevention is tailored to destination, age, duration, and activities. Routine antibiotics are not a general prophylaxis strategy. Vaccines and preventive medicines may be needed. Insect protection and food and water safety address different transmission routes.

Chapter 30: Infection in Immunocompromised and Medically Complex Children

Question 1
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Neutropenic fever is a clinical emergency, and counts may change. Evaluation follows the host, examination, cultures, and protocol. A normal count does not erase the risk. Discharge without assessment is unsafe.
Question 2
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Different immune defects create different vulnerability patterns. T cells are important for viral, fungal, and intracellular control, while antibody defects increase risk from encapsulated bacteria. Prevention should be tailored rather than identical for every child.
Question 3
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Complex children need written escalation, device, and prophylaxis plans with clear ownership. Hospital discharge is not the end of infection risk. Preventive medicines should be managed with the specialty team. Verbal instructions alone are fragile in a high-risk home setting.
